Autumn olive, multiflora rose, and overgrown edges

Autumn olive and multiflora rose show up all over North Central West Virginia. They fill in old pastures, climb fence lines, and take over the edge of a wood lot. Both are woody enough that a regular mower or a brush hog often cannot keep up once the plants are established. A forestry mulcher can grind that growth in place and leave the ground open enough to walk, mow, or graze again.

This page is about mechanical clearing and mulching only. It is not a treatment plan, and it does not involve pesticides or a claim that the plants will never come back. Invasives can resprout. Many landowners follow mulching with regular mowing, grazing, or a plan they work out with a local extension office or a land manager.

What to tell the crew

If you already know the plants, name them. If you only know that a field or a fence line has turned into a thicket, photos are enough to start. Share the acreage, whether the growth is in the open or mixed into woods you want to keep, and whether a creek or a steep bank is part of the job.

This work often overlaps with brush clearing and with bringing pasture back on a farm. If the same property also needs a trail or a homesite opened, include that in one quote request.
