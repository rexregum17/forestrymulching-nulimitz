What is forestry mulching?

Forestry mulching is a way to clear land with a machine that has a spinning drum, often called a mulching head. The drum grinds brush, saplings, and smaller trees into wood chips. Those chips are usually left on the ground, which means less hauling, no burn pile, and less disturbance of the topsoil than you often get with a bulldozer.

That is why landowners around Morgantown, Fairmont, Bridgeport, and Clarksburg use it to open woods, clear underbrush, cut a trail, or get a lot ready for a house or a pasture. If you want a closer look at a specific job, see brush clearing, lot clearing, or trail clearing.

Forestry mulching compared with traditional land clearing

Traditional land clearing often relies on bulldozers or excavators to push trees and dirt. That can be the right tool when large trees have to come down, stumps have to be pulled, or a site has to be graded for a foundation. It can also leave piles, bare soil, and a cleanup job after the machines leave.

Forestry mulching is a better fit when you want the growth gone and the ground still walkable, with a layer of chips to help hold the soil. The two methods can be used on the same property. Describe what you want the land to be ready for, and a crew can tell you which approach fits.

Forestry mulching compared with brush hogging

A brush hog, sometimes called a bush hog, is a mower made for grass, weeds, and light woody stems in a field that is already open. It is a farm tool, and it does that job well. It is not made for thick underbrush, saplings, or small trees in the woods, and it will not grind stumps or leave a finished chip layer in timber.

A forestry mulcher is built for denser, woodier growth. If a field has only grass and light brush, a brush hog may be enough. If the growth is woody, tall, or standing in timber, mulching is usually the method that can actually get through it.

What the job looks like on the ground

Most crews will want to know the acreage, how thick the growth is, the size of the trees, the slope, and how a machine would get in. A mulcher can usually handle brush, saplings, and smaller trees, and it can grind smaller stumps down close to grade as it works. Larger mature trees often need to be cut first, or they can be left standing for a selective clearing job.

The mulch usually stays on the ground. It can help with erosion and will break down over time. If you need a thinner layer so you can seed grass or pour a driveway, say so. Work can often be done in winter when the ground is firm or frozen. Wet spells and deep mud can delay a start. Steep or uneven ground is common in this part of West Virginia, and it should be mentioned up front.

After the machine leaves, many people seed, let a pasture come back, or simply walk the land while the chips settle. Brush can return over time, especially autumn olive and multiflora rose. How fast that happens depends on the plants that were there and whether the ground is mowed or grazed afterward.
